# Second Pair Editorial — Author Problems Library

## Purpose

This is an internal working library built from the supplied alpha/beta-reading, critique, reader-interaction and developmental-feedback corpus. It turns recurring manuscript concerns into plain-language questions that can guide the website, intake, sample reads, services, newsletter, social posts, community replies and future resources.

This is not a survey and should not be presented as statistical research. The corpus is a qualitative source of real author/reader/editor language and examples.

## Core rule

Do not sell the service first. Start with the problem the author is actually trying to solve.

Use:

**author concern → reader reaction → evidence on the page → possible cause → priority → author's decision**

## Problem library

### 1. The opening is not holding attention
**Author language:** “My opening feels slow.” “Does the first chapter work?” “I don't know if people will keep reading.”

**Look for:** delayed stakes, too much setup before a reason to continue, unclear situation, weak scene purpose, explanation before the reader has a reason to care.

**Likely services:** Beta Reading, Manuscript Diagnostic, Developmental Editing.

**Sample-read question:** Where did you first want to keep reading? Where did your attention dip?

**Content ideas:** First-chapter audit; why an opening can be technically good but still slow; hook versus reason to care.

### 2. The story feels slow, but the cause is unclear
**Author language:** “The pacing is slow.” “I know something drags.” “I don't know what to cut.”

**Look for:** repeated beats, scenes without a clear job, delayed consequences, exposition interrupting action, weak cause-and-effect, progression that does not match the intended reading experience.

**Likely services:** Manuscript Diagnostic, Developmental Editing, Beta Reading.

**Sample-read question:** What changed, mattered or became harder during each scene?

### 3. Characters make sense to the author but may not make sense to the reader
**Author language:** “My character is supposed to be likeable.” “I know why they did it.” “Does the motivation come across?”

**Look for:** missing motivation, choices that appear arbitrary, passive protagonists, unclear emotional turns, consequences that do not follow from choices.

**Likely services:** Beta Reading, Manuscript Diagnostic, Developmental Editing.

### 4. Characters feel flat or too similar
**Author language:** “Everyone sounds the same.” “The characters don't have enough personality.”

**Look for:** similar dialogue rhythms, identical vocabulary, repeated reactions, weak wants, interchangeable choices, narration that does not feel tied to viewpoint.

**Likely services:** Beta Reading, Developmental Editing, Line Editing.

### 5. Dialogue does not sound natural
**Author language:** “Does this dialogue sound real?” “My dialogue feels stiff.”

**Look for:** characters explaining what they already know, exposition disguised as conversation, identical voices, dialogue that does not change the situation, excessive greetings and filler.

**Likely services:** Beta Reading, Line Editing, Developmental Editing.

### 6. Worldbuilding is clear in the author's head but unclear on the page
**Author language:** “I know the world makes sense.” “I am worried readers won't understand it.”

**Look for:** missing context, unexplained terms, information arriving too early or too late, infodumps, unclear rules, too many names or concepts at once.

**Likely services:** Beta Reading, Manuscript Diagnostic, Developmental Editing.

### 7. The reader is confused about what is happening
**Author language:** “Is this confusing?” “I don't know if I explained this well enough.”

**Look for:** unclear referents, missing transitions, viewpoint jumps, chronology problems, skipped causal steps, information the reader needs arriving after the moment it matters.

**Likely services:** Beta Reading, Manuscript Diagnostic, Copy Editing for local clarity issues.

### 8. The prose is technically fine but the story feels flat
**Author language:** “The writing is okay, but it has no energy.” “It reads fine but doesn't hit.”

**Look for:** emotional distance, repetitive sentence movement, generic description, weak scene-level tension, prose that calls attention to itself or does not support the scene.

**Likely services:** Beta Reading, Line Editing, Developmental Editing.

### 9. Feedback is coming in, but the author still does not know what to change
**Author language:** “I got feedback but I don't know what to fix first.” “Everyone tells me something different.”

**Look for:** symptoms being mistaken for causes, conflicting reader preferences, missing priority, feedback without examples, several comments pointing to the same deeper issue.

**Likely services:** Manuscript Diagnostic, Developmental Editing, Revision Review.

### 10. The author disagrees with a reader and does not know what to do with the feedback
**Author language:** “But that is not what I meant.” “The reader misunderstood this.”

**Useful response:** Separate intention from reader experience. The author may keep the choice. The useful question is why the reader reached that interpretation and whether the gap matters for the intended audience.

**Likely services:** Beta Reading, Manuscript Diagnostic, Revision Review.

### 11. Revision may have fixed one problem while creating another
**Author language:** “I changed it, but now something else feels wrong.” “Did the revision actually work?”

**Look for:** new pacing gaps, continuity problems, lost character motivation, setup/payoff changes, repeated information, scenes that no longer lead naturally into one another.

**Likely services:** Revision Review.

### 12. The author is unsure whether they need a reader or an editor
**Author language:** “Should I get a beta reader or developmental editor?” “Do I need a full edit?”

**Useful response:** Ask what question they need answered. A reader can tell them how the story lands. A diagnostic can help find a problem. Developmental editing is for deeper story-level revision. Line/copy editing is for settled prose and local issues.

**Likely services:** Route according to the question, not the size of the manuscript.

### 13. Genre expectations may be working for or against the manuscript
**Author language:** “Readers of this genre expect X.” “Will this work for my audience?”

**Look for:** promises made by the blurb/opening, expected pace, genre conventions, reader expectations and whether the manuscript intentionally departs from them.

**Important boundary:** Genre-specific observations should be tied to the actual target market. Do not treat one community's preferences as universal writing rules.

### 14. The author wants honesty but does not want vague or destructive feedback
**Author language:** “Be brutal.” “Tell me if it is bad.” “I want honest feedback.”

**Second Pair standard:** Honest does not mean harsh. Useful feedback explains what happened during the read, where it happened, why it may matter and what the author might consider next.

### 15. The author wants a reader to notice problems without rewriting the book for them
**Author language:** “I don't want someone to rewrite my voice.”

**Second Pair standard:** Keep creative ownership with the author. Identify problems, explain them and offer possible directions without replacing the author's prose or decisions.

## Sample-read question bank

Use a small selection rather than asking every question every time:

- Where did you first feel pulled into the story?
- Where did your attention dip?
- Was there a moment you had to reread because something was unclear?
- Which character did you understand most easily, and why?
- Did any character choice feel unmotivated from the reader's point of view?
- Did the dialogue feel distinct between characters?
- Was there any explanation you wanted earlier or later?
- What question were you most interested in having answered?
- Did the scene change in a meaningful way by the end?
- Was there a point where you wanted the story to move faster?

## Intake prompts

Instead of asking only “What service do you need?”, invite the author to choose what is closest:

- I want to know how the story lands with a reader.
- Something feels wrong and I cannot find the cause.
- I know what needs work and want help planning the revision.
- I revised the manuscript and want to know whether the changes worked.
- The story is settled and I want help with the prose.
- I mainly need grammar, consistency or final corrections.
- I am not sure yet.
