# Second Pair Editorial — Client Journey Operations

## Purpose

This document turns the editorial knowledge system into a practical client journey from first contact through completed work and follow-up.

The aim is simple: make it easy for an author to explain what they need, easy for us to understand the real problem, and easy for both sides to agree on the right scope before work begins.

## Journey

`DISCOVERY → INQUIRY → SAMPLE/CONVERSATION → ASSESSMENT → SERVICE ROUTING → SCOPE → QUOTE → DECISION → BOOKING → HANDOFF → EDITORIAL WORK → DELIVERY → FEEDBACK → FOLLOW-UP`

Not every client needs every stage. A returning client, for example, may go directly from inquiry to scope.

## 1. Discovery

The author may arrive through:
- a website page
- a checklist or other resource
- a Facebook or other community discussion
- a newsletter
- a referral
- a direct message

The goal at this stage is not to sell. It is to make the problem easier to understand.

## 2. Inquiry

Record:
- name
- email
- title
- genre
- word count
- manuscript stage
- what the author wants from the read
- service they have in mind
- their own description of the problem
- requested sample
- source of inquiry when known

Do not treat the selected service as the final diagnosis.

## 3. Sample / Conversation

Use the sample when the question is about fit, reader response, or whether the manuscript needs a particular kind of help.

A sample is not a free miniature version of a paid edit. Keep it useful, specific and limited.

## 4. Assessment

Before recommending a service, answer:

1. What is the author asking us to help with?
2. What can we actually observe from the material provided?
3. Is the concern local or does it point to a larger pattern?
4. What kind of work would answer the author's question?
5. Is a smaller service enough?
6. What information is still missing before quoting?

## 5. Service routing

### READ
Use Alpha or Beta Reading when the main question is about the reading experience.

### DIAGNOSE
Use Manuscript Diagnostic when the author knows something is wrong but needs help finding the cause.

### DEVELOP
Use Developmental Editing when the manuscript needs substantial story-level revision guidance.

### REFINE
Use Line or Copy Editing when the story is settled and the main need is prose or technical correction.

### POLISH
Use Proofreading for the final error-catching pass after editing is complete.

### REVISE
Use Revision Review when the author has already revised and wants to know whether the important problems were actually addressed.

### COMPLETE
Use Complete Manuscript Development when several stages are genuinely needed. Do not use it simply because it has a larger scope.

## 6. Scope before price

Before giving a confirmed quote, record:
- service
- manuscript word count
- exact deliverables
- whether one or multiple passes are included
- whether comments/report are included
- expected start window
- expected delivery window if agreed
- anything explicitly outside scope

Do not promise a turnaround until capacity and scope have been checked.

## 7. Quote

A quote should state:
- what we are doing
- what the author receives
- price or pricing basis
- what is not included
- any assumptions
- what the author needs to provide
- how to accept the project

Avoid vague language such as “full support” unless the actual deliverables are listed.

## 8. Decision and booking

Once the author accepts:
- confirm scope in writing
- confirm files/version
- confirm price
- confirm payment terms
- confirm start and delivery dates
- identify the best contact email
- record any special concerns

No work should begin on a materially different scope without confirming the change.

## 9. Handoff

The internal handoff should contain:
- client name
- manuscript title
- service
- word count
- agreed deliverables
- agreed dates
- author's stated concerns
- known reader questions
- important style or project notes
- previous feedback or revisions when supplied

## 10. Delivery

Delivery should explain what is attached and what the author should look at first.

For a report-heavy service, identify:
- main finding
- top priorities
- supporting examples
- what is optional
- what the author should decide next

Do not bury the most useful information under a long introduction.

## 11. Feedback

Ask for feedback about the service, not for praise.

Useful questions:
- Did the feedback answer the question you came in with?
- Was anything unclear?
- Was the level of detail useful?
- Did the comments help you decide what to do next?
- Was there anything you expected that was missing?

Do not ask clients to manufacture testimonials.

## 12. Follow-up

Follow-up should depend on where the manuscript is now.

Examples:
- After a beta read: offer help interpreting or prioritising feedback only if needed.
- After a diagnostic: check whether the author wants to discuss the findings or plan the next stage.
- After developmental editing: check whether the revision plan is clear.
- After revision review: identify whether another revision or a prose pass is appropriate.
- After line/copy editing: discuss proofreading if the manuscript is ready for it.

A follow-up is useful when it helps the author take the next sensible step. It should not be a generic “just checking in” sequence.

## Decision rule

When in doubt:

**Answer the author's question first. Sell the service second.**
