# How do I know whether my revision actually worked?

**Canonical problem:** `/problems/revision-worked`

You changed the opening because readers said it was slow. You added a conversation because the character's motive was unclear. You cut several pages of explanation. The manuscript looks different, but you are still not sure it reads better.

## Start with the reason you made the change

Before you compare versions, write down the problem you were trying to fix in one sentence. For example: *Readers could not tell why Mara refused to leave the station.* That is more useful than “improve chapter four”.

Then identify the change you made. Did you add a clue earlier? Give Mara a clearer goal? Move the conversation? Remove information that distracted from the decision? Be specific enough that you can check whether the revision had the intended effect.

## Compare what the reader can now understand

Read the old and new versions without editing either one. At the moment that caused trouble before, ask what information is now available. Can a reader follow the character's choice? Does the new version answer the intended question without explaining three things that were meant to stay mysterious?

A revision can improve one problem and create another. The new clue may make the scene clearer but reveal the mystery too early. The shorter opening may move faster but remove the relationship that makes the later conflict matter.

## Change one main thing at a time when you can

If you alter the dialogue, scene order, character motivation and point of view together, you may not know which change helped. You do not have to make every revision tiny, but keep track of the main reason for each substantial change.

Also check the neighbouring chapters. A scene may now work on its own while creating a continuity gap or making the next chapter repeat information.

## Ask someone who has not memorised the old draft

Authors become familiar with both versions. You know what the new sentence is supposed to clarify, so it can be difficult to notice whether it actually does. A fresh reader can tell you what they understand now, where they still hesitate and whether the original reaction has changed.

Do not ask only, “Is it better?” Ask them to describe what they now think is happening at the point that used to cause trouble. Their explanation gives you something more specific to compare.

**A revision record helps:** problem noticed → change made → effect expected → what the new read shows → side effects to check.

**When another read may help:** A revision review is useful when the manuscript has changed substantially and you need to know whether the intended improvement is visible to someone who is not carrying your own explanation of the new draft.
