# Second Pair Editorial — Editorial Knowledge System

## Purpose

This is the shared working system for turning real author concerns from the editorial corpus into consistent decisions across the business.

It is not a claim that every author has the same problem. The corpus is qualitative source material. Use it to generate questions, not rules.

## Core loop

**AUTHOR WORDS → READER EXPERIENCE → PAGE EVIDENCE → PATTERN → LIKELY CAUSE → PRIORITY → AUTHOR DECISION**

Do not jump from an author's complaint straight to a service recommendation.

## 1. Author problem layer

Start with the words the author actually uses:

- “The opening feels slow.”
- “Something is wrong.”
- “My character feels flat.”
- “Everyone sounds the same.”
- “Readers keep getting confused.”
- “I got feedback but I still don't know what to fix.”
- “I revised it and I'm not sure it worked.”
- “I don't know whether I need a beta reader or an editor.”

Do not replace these with jargon unless the jargon helps the author understand the problem.

## 2. Reader-experience layer

Ask what the reader actually experiences:

- Where did attention rise or fall?
- Where did the reader become curious?
- Where did they hesitate or reread?
- What did they understand differently from what the author intended?
- What did they expect next?
- Which character choices felt clear or unclear?
- What information arrived too early, too late or at the wrong moment?

## 3. Evidence layer

Point to the actual page. A useful observation should be traceable to a scene, passage, choice, pattern or sequence.

Avoid conclusions such as “the pacing is bad” without showing what created that reaction.

## 4. Pattern layer

One awkward paragraph is not automatically a manuscript-level problem.

Ask whether the same thing happens:

- once
- in several scenes
- across one character arc
- across multiple viewpoints
- throughout the manuscript

## 5. Cause layer

Symptoms and causes are not always the same.

Example:

**Symptom:** “The middle is slow.”

Possible causes include repeated information, scenes that do not change the situation, delayed consequences, weak cause-and-effect, or a character who is reacting instead of making choices.

Do not diagnose a cause without enough evidence.

## 6. Priority layer

Not every issue deserves equal attention.

Prioritize according to:

1. effect on the reading experience
2. how often the issue appears
3. whether fixing it affects other parts of the book
4. whether the issue blocks later revision
5. whether the author actually wants to make that change

## 7. Service routing

| Author question | Possible starting point |
|---|---|
| “How does this story land?” | Beta Reading |
| “Is the developing story working?” | Alpha Reading |
| “Something is wrong; what is it?” | Manuscript Diagnostic |
| “What needs to change?” | Developmental Editing |
| “Did my revision work?” | Revision Review |
| “How can the prose read better?” | Line Editing |
| “Can you fix grammar and consistency?” | Copy Editing |
| “Is this clean enough for publication?” | Proofreading |
| “I'm not sure.” | Sample Read / conversation first |

The manuscript's size alone should not determine the service.

## 8. Sample-read protocol

A sample read should demonstrate how Second Pair thinks, not give away the entire paid service.

Use a small set of questions:

- Where did I want to keep reading?
- Where did my attention dip?
- Was anything unclear?
- Did the character's choice make sense from what I knew at the time?
- Did the scene change something?
- What question was I carrying forward?
- What is the most useful thing to tell the author from these pages?

A strong sample response normally contains:

**reaction → page evidence → why it matters → possible direction → what the author can decide**

## 9. Client intake

Ask about the concern before asking the author to select an editorial label.

Useful intake choices:

- I want to know how the story lands with a reader.
- Something feels wrong and I cannot find the cause.
- I know what needs work and want help planning the revision.
- I revised the manuscript and want to know whether the changes worked.
- The story is settled and I want help with the prose.
- I mainly need grammar, consistency or final corrections.
- I'm not sure yet.

## 10. Content engine

One author problem can become several useful pieces without repeating the same copy:

**PROBLEM → SHORT POST → DEEPER ARTICLE → NEWSLETTER → COMMUNITY ANSWER → FAQ → CHECKLIST ITEM → SAMPLE-READ QUESTION → SERVICE PAGE EXAMPLE**

Change the purpose for each platform. Do not copy and paste the same paragraph everywhere.

## 11. Community response standard

When answering an author's public question:

1. Answer the question first.
2. Explain the underlying issue only when useful.
3. Give one practical thing they can check.
4. Do not diagnose an unseen manuscript.
5. Mention a service only when the context makes it relevant.
6. Do not force a DM or sales pitch.

## 12. Newsletter standard

A useful issue should normally contain:

- one real author problem
- a concrete example
- what a reader may experience
- what the author can check
- one practical takeaway
- a light invitation to explore the relevant resource or sample read

## 13. Business rule

The corpus should make the business more specific, not more complicated.

Do not add a new service merely because a problem appears in the corpus. First ask whether the problem is already handled by an existing service and whether authors are actively looking for a separate offer.

## 14. Voice rule

Use normal professional language.

Prefer:

- “what the reader sees” over “reader-facing dynamics”
- “what is not working” over “pain point”
- “what to fix first” over “prioritisation framework”
- “a closer look” over “deep-dive assessment”
- “help with the revision” over “revision optimization”

Avoid inflated marketing language, generic AI phrasing and invented certainty.
