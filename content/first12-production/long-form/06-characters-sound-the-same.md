# Why do my characters sound the same?

**Canonical problem:** `/problems/characters-sound-the-same`

If every character sounds alike, changing a few favourite words may not be enough. Voice is not just accent, catchphrases or how often someone says “well”. It also comes from what a person notices, what they avoid, how quickly they answer and what they think is worth saying.

## Listen to what each character pays attention to

Give several characters the same situation: a stranger enters the room carrying a wet coat and a locked box. One character may notice the weapon-shaped weight of the box. Another sees the expensive fabric. A third worries about the water on the floor. Their dialogue can be simple and still feel different because their attention is different.

If they all notice the same details, make the same jokes and reach the same conclusions, the problem may begin in characterisation rather than sentence style.

## Compare reactions, not just dialogue samples

Choose a scene where two or three characters speak together. Remove the names and dialogue tags. Can you tell who is speaking? If not, put the tags back and ask a second question: do they react differently when they hear something uncomfortable, surprising or threatening?

A guarded character may answer a direct question with another question. A person who wants approval may over-explain. Someone who is impatient may skip the social opening and go straight to the point. These patterns should grow from the person, not be assigned as a decorative quirk.

## Let the situation change the voice too

People do not speak in exactly the same way with a friend, a parent, a supervisor and a stranger. Stress can shorten one person's sentences and make another talk too much. A character may be funny in public and blunt in private. Consistency does not mean sameness across every situation.

## A quick test for your next edit

Pick one exchange. Write a short note for each speaker: what do they want from this conversation, what are they trying not to reveal and what do they notice first? Then revise only the lines that do not fit those answers.

Do not force a verbal tic into every line. If a character's word choices are distinctive but their priorities are identical to everyone else's, the voices may still blur.

**When another read may help:** Ask a reader which character said a line before they look at the dialogue tag. Their answer can show whether the voices are distinct on the page, not just in your head.
